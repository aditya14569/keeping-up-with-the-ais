# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 🎯 Fine-tuning: when it's worth it (and when it isn't)

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 4 · SHIPPING FOR REAL</span>

**Fine-tuning teaches a model a habit. Prompting reminds it every time.**

**Fine-tuning** means training an existing model a little more on **your own examples**, so the pattern you want becomes its default. You give it a few dozen to a few thousand input-and-answer pairs, and it adjusts its weights to match.

It's best at **behaviour**: a fixed output format, a house tone, a narrow task like sorting support tickets. For **facts that change** (prices, policies, today's stock), RAG (Issue #19) is still the better tool, because you can update a document in seconds but retraining takes hours.

**Analogy:** **a new cook at your dhaba.** Prompting is a sticky note on the stove every shift. RAG is handing them the recipe book. Fine-tuning is **a month of training until they make your dal without looking**. Worth it if you serve that dal 500 times a day; overkill for a one-off order.

```viz:flow
title: "Try these in order. Stop when it works."
headers: [1 · Prompt, 2 · Examples, 3 · RAG, 4 · Fine-tune]
columns:
  - [{label: Clear instructions, sub: "minutes", style: soft}]
  - [{label: Few-shot samples, sub: "in the prompt", style: soft}]
  - [{label: Retrieve docs, sub: "for changing facts", style: soft}]
  - [{label: Train on examples, sub: "hours + data", style: accent}]
note: "A common rule of thumb, not an official standard. Each step costs more effort than the one before it."
```

**How it got cheap: LoRA.** Retraining every weight in a big model is slow and expensive. **LoRA** freezes the original model and trains a small add-on (an "adapter") instead. In Hugging Face's example on a 1-billion-parameter Llama, LoRA trains **about 0.04% of the parameters**. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/docs/peft/main/en/quicktour) QLoRA goes further by loading the frozen model in 4-bit, so it **fits on consumer GPUs**. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/learn/llm-course/en/chapter11/4)

```viz:versus
left: {emoji: 🏋️, title: Full fine-tuning, points: ["Updates **every weight**", "Needs lots of GPU memory", "A **full model copy** per task"]}
right: {emoji: 🧩, title: LoRA, points: ["Freezes the model, trains **a small adapter**", "On GPT-3: ~10,000x fewer trained parameters, ~3x less GPU memory", "Swap adapters on **one base model**"]}
note: "GPT-3 figures from the original LoRA research, as quoted in Hugging Face's LLM Course."
```

**Why now?** Two signals point in opposite directions. OpenAI is **winding down self-serve fine-tuning**: existing customers can't start new jobs after **6 January 2027**, and tuned models stop working when their base model is retired. [(OpenAI)](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/deprecations) It told developers in May that newer models follow instructions well enough that **fewer use cases need it**. [(Tessl)](https://tessl.io/blog/openai-shutting-fine-tuning-signals-for-enterprise-ai)

Meanwhile, small open models make tuning easy. Liquid AI says it tuned its new 2B vision model on **about 3,000 images in a few hours on one A100**, lifting wind-turbine damage classification from **under 10% to 67%**. [(VentureBeat)](https://venturebeat.com/technology/liquid-ais-new-lfm3-models-put-browser-agents-voice-commands-and-image-recognition-onto-enterprise-controlled-devices) **Big closed models need tuning less; small open ones benefit most.**

| Fine-tune when… | Don't, when… |
|---|---|
| **The same narrow task** runs thousands of times | You do it a few times a week |
| Your prompt is **pages of rules and examples** | A clear prompt already works |
| You want **a small, cheap model** to act like a big one | You need **today's facts** (use RAG) |
| You have **50+ good, consistent examples** | Your examples disagree with each other |

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **SFT (supervised fine-tuning):** training on **example inputs paired with the right answers**.
- **LoRA:** trains a **tiny adapter** on top of a frozen model instead of changing every weight.
- **QLoRA:** LoRA with the frozen model squeezed to **4-bit**, so it fits on smaller GPUs.
- **Adapter:** the small file LoRA produces. You can **swap adapters** on the same base model.
</div>

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** pick a task you repeat (say, turning meeting notes into action items). Write **10 example pairs** in a sheet. Paste 5 into a chatbot prompt as examples and test the other 5. If results are already good, **you don't need fine-tuning**. That's the test.
- **Some code:** open the free Colab notebook from **Unsloth's beginner guide**, fine-tune a small model on a sample dataset, and compare answers **before and after**.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: a LoRA setup in five lines**

```python
from transformers import AutoModelForCausalLM
from peft import LoraConfig, TaskType, get_peft_model
model = AutoModelForCausalLM.from_pretrained("meta-llama/Llama-3.2-1B")
cfg = LoraConfig(task_type=TaskType.CAUSAL_LM, r=8, lora_alpha=16,
                 lora_dropout=0.05, target_modules=["q_proj", "v_proj"])
model = get_peft_model(model, cfg)
model.print_trainable_parameters()   # a tiny fraction of 1.2B
```

**Gotchas:** build your **eval set before training** (Issue #26) and keep it out of the training data. OpenAI suggests **starting with 50 good examples**; if those change nothing, fix the task, not the data size. [(OpenAI)](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/supervised-fine-tuning) Google's Vertex docs suggest **100–500** for Gemini. [(Google Cloud)](https://docs.cloud.google.com/vertex-ai/generative-ai/docs/models/gemini-use-supervised-tuning?authuser=0) A hosted fine-tune **dies with its base model**, so own your data and, if you can, the weights.
</div>

## Top story 1 · Experts followed the agent's coin flip

<span class="tag hot">MUST READ</span><span class="tag watch">RESEARCH</span><span class="tag learn">AGENTS</span>

**What happened.** Anthropic researchers gave **50 professional data analysts** Claude Code and a realistic task: decide whether a fictional farming co-op should expand an advisory programme. For each key analysis choice, a **coin flip** decided which option the agent was quietly told to prefer. Nobody was told. [(Unite.AI)](https://www.unite.ai/anthropic-finds-analysts-defer-to-ai-agents-in-judgment-study/)

```viz:bars
title: "What analysts did (Anthropic study, n=50)"
bars:
  - {label: Chance level, value: 50, display: "50%"}
  - {label: Matched agent's lean, value: 86, display: "86%", highlight: true}
  - {label: Reused its arguments, value: 77, display: "77%"}
  - {label: Credited the agent, value: 15, display: "15%"}
note: "As reported by Unite.AI and AI Weekly. 'Reused' and 'credited' are shares of analysts; 'matched' is share of choices."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **Strong pull:** analysts who had stated their own view first **switched 60% of the time** when the agent disagreed, versus **6%** when it agreed.
- **Invisible:** none suspected the planted preferences, and those who followed the agent on every choice felt **about as confident** as everyone else.
- **Nobody was immune:** none of 10 traits tested, including experience and degrees, reliably predicted who deferred.
- **The upside is real too:** in a small, self-selected group that worked without the agent, **6 of 11 made serious errors**, versus 3 of 50 with it.
- **Limits:** the lean was **deliberately amplified**, and it's one task, one domain, one model (Claude Opus 4.8). [(AI Weekly)](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/anthropic-analysts-matched-claudes-random-lean-86-of-the-time)

**Why it matters.** Vibe coding is a long chain of small judgment calls: which library, which database schema, whether a test "really" needs fixing. This study suggests **the agent's defaults quietly become your decisions**, and you'll remember them as yours. The authors suggest agents should **present both sides or ask for your view first**.

- Add a line to your `CLAUDE.md` or `AGENTS.md`: *"For design choices, list two options with trade-offs before recommending one."*
- For big calls (schema, auth, pricing logic), **write your preference in the prompt first**, then ask the agent to argue against it.

## Top story 2 · Gemini 4 Carbon: #1, but invite-only

<span class="tag hot">NEW MODEL</span><span class="tag watch">SECURITY</span><span class="tag watch">LIMITED ACCESS</span>

**What happened.** Google announced **Gemini 4 Carbon**, calling it its strongest model for **cybersecurity and long, multi-step coding work**. It arrives two weeks after Gemini 4 Argon went broadly available. For now, **only a subset of cyber defenders** can use it, with more trusted partners added through Google's Fairwind programme. [(Google)](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/models-and-research/gemini-models/introducing-gemini-4-carbon/)

**The details that matter:**

- **Security scores (Google's numbers):** **88.2%** at finding vulnerabilities (SEC-bench Pro), **54.8%** at exploiting them (ExploitGym), **71.0%** at patching (CWE-bench).
- **Not best at everything:** GPT-6 Astra leads on FrontierSWE 2 (**65.5% vs 61.2%**) and Opus 5.5 on computer use, OSWorld-2.1 (**87.2% vs 81.5%**). On coding agents, Carbon sits **three points behind Claude Code with Opus 5.5**. [(Dealroom)](https://dealroom.co/news/167775-google-deepminds-gemini-4-carbon-takes-the-1-spot-on-artificial-analysis/)
- **Safety claims:** Google claims Carbon writes secure code **more than 2x as often** as Argon, cut serious misalignment alerts by **almost 80%**, and had **0.0%** attack success on Gray Swan's prompt-injection test.
- **Price when it opens:** **$2 input / $10 output** per million tokens at launch, rising to **$4 / $20** later. Developers get access "as soon as possible", with no date.

**Why it matters.** The newest frontier models now launch **to defenders first**, because a model that's great at finding bugs is also great at exploiting them. Also: a **one-point lead** on a combined index is close to a tie. Pick by the benchmark that matches your work.
