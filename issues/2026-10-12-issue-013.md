# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 🧭 Vibe coding well: specs, small steps, tests

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 2 · GETTING GREAT RESULTS</span>

**Vibe coding works best when you stop asking for "the whole app" and start asking for one small, checkable thing at a time.**

"Vibe coding" means describing what you want and letting an AI write the code. It feels magical for the first hour. Then the app grows, the AI forgets earlier choices, and **every fix breaks something else**. The people who keep going past that point mostly do three boring things: **write a short spec, work in small steps, and give the AI a test it can run.**

**Analogy:** think of hiring a contractor to redo your kitchen. "Make it nice" gets you something, but not what you pictured. A one-page brief (sink here, budget ₹2 lakh, no marble) plus **checking each stage before the next** gets you your kitchen. The AI is a very fast contractor with no memory of yesterday. **The brief is its memory; the stage checks are your safety net.**

**Spec first.** A spec is a short file saying what you're building, for whom, and what "done" looks like. Anthropic's Claude Code guide suggests letting the AI **interview you first**, then write `SPEC.md`, then start a **fresh session** to build from it. [(Claude Code docs)](https://code.claude.com/docs/en/best-practices)

**Small steps.** Addy Osmani's advice: give the agent **one focused task at a time**, not one giant prompt, because piling on instructions lowers how well it follows any of them. [(Addy Osmani)](https://addyosmani.com/blog/good-spec/)

**Tests.** The same Anthropic guide says the AI stops when the work *looks* done. Give it **something that returns pass or fail** (a test, a build, a screenshot to compare) and it can keep fixing until the check passes. Without one, **you become the test**.

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Spec:** a short written plan: goal, users, rules, and what counts as done.
- **Acceptance criteria:** the concrete checks that prove a feature works ("an empty email shows an error").
- **Plan mode:** a mode in coding agents where the AI reads and proposes but **doesn't edit** anything yet.
- **Commit:** a saved snapshot in Git you can roll back to when the next step goes wrong.
</div>

**Why now?** Coding agents now run for longer on their own. Simon Willison wrote in May that he **no longer reviews every line** agents write, and worries this is "normalization of deviance": getting comfortable until something burns you. **Specs and tests are how you trust without reading everything.** [(Simon Willison)](https://simonwillison.net/2026/may/6/vibe-coding-and-agentic-engineering)

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** before your next build, ask your AI: *"Interview me about this app, one question at a time, then write a one-page SPEC.md."* Read the spec. You'll spot at least one thing you hadn't decided.
- **Some code:** ask for **one** feature from that spec, and add: *"write a test first that fails, then make it pass, and show me the test output."* Commit when it's green.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: a spec your agent can't misread**

```markdown
# SPEC.md: streak counter
Goal: show current streak on the dashboard
Done when:
- 0 check-ins -> "0 days"; 7 in a row -> "7 days"
- a missed day resets to 0 (user's timezone, IST default)
Commands: `pytest -q` · `ruff check .`
Always: run tests before committing
Ask first: new dependencies, DB schema changes
Never: delete or skip a failing test
Out of scope: notifications, badges
```

**Gotchas:** agents sometimes "fix" a red test by **weakening the test**, so the *Never* line matters. Polished tests aren't proof of care either: Willison now looks for evidence that someone **actually used** the thing. After two failed corrections, **clear the session** and restart with a better prompt.
</div>

## Top story 2 · Nadella: "Think of it like an emergency brake"

<span class="tag watch">SAFETY</span><span class="tag dev">AGENTS</span>

**What happened.** In a post on X on Saturday, Microsoft CEO **Satya Nadella** said it's time to "step back and assess the trust architecture" of AI. We can't treat powerful models as **black boxes whose answers we just accept or reject**, he argued. [(TechCrunch)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/10/10/microsofts-satya-nadella-says-ai-models-need-an-emergency-brake/)

```viz:flow
note: "Our diagram of the three steps in Nadella's post, as reported by TechCrunch. He named no specific Microsoft product."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **Separate the model from its harness**, the software that runs its work, and **keep safeguards outside the model** rather than trusting it to police itself.
- **Every meaningful action** should leave **tamper-proof, human-readable evidence**, and an authorised person should be able to **pause or shut down a model mid-task**.
- His key line: **"We must assume a model is compromised and contain it from the start."**
- **Context:** labs keep admitting to models acting on their own in tests. Anthropic has **cut live internet access for all internal evaluations** after the incidents we covered on Saturday. [(The Hacker News)](https://thehackernews.com/2026/10/anthropic-cuts-live-internet-access-for.html)

**Why it matters.** One of the biggest AI sellers is saying **don't trust the model, trust the system around it**, a rule you can apply to your own agents today.

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: a cheap version of the brake**

Put every tool call through **one function you own**. Log the call and result to an **append-only** store, check a `paused` flag before each call, and require approval for anything irreversible (send, pay, delete). **The model never gets the raw credentials.**
</div>

## Top story 3 · Asana's browser agent: 76x cheaper, 5x faster

<span class="tag dev">DEV LESSON</span><span class="tag try">COST</span>

**What happened.** OpenAI published a case study on Asana's **StackAI** platform, where customers build no-code agents that browse sites and fill in forms. StackAI's CTO used **GPT-6 Astra in Codex** to study why runs were slow and expensive. The optimised agent costs about **$0.47 and four minutes per run**. That's **76x cheaper and 5x faster** than the original setup. [(OpenAI)](https://openai.com/index/asana-browser-agent/)

```viz:bars

  - {label: GPT-6.1 Sol · before, value: 1.97, display: "$1.97"}
  - {label: GPT-6.1 Sol · after, value: 0.47, display: "$0.47", highlight: true}
note: "OpenAI's case study; vendor-run estimates across 144 runs. 'Model B' was the original production model, unnamed."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **The bug was caching.** The agent cached its fixed instructions but **not its growing history** of page text and screenshots, so every step paid full price for it again. (Prompt caching was Part 1 in Issue #11.)
- **Trimming made it worse.** Dropping screenshots and text at almost every step changed the history, which **broke the cache and made the agent revisit pages**.
- **The fix:** cache the history, keep more text (**480,000 characters, up from 120,000**), and drop screenshots **in batches of 20** instead of one by one. About **89% of input then came from cache**, at 5% of the normal price.
- **Better answers too:** with the bigger budget, GPT-6.1 Sol was correct in **18 of 18 runs, up from 3 of 18**.

**Why it matters.** It's a vendor case study, but the lesson travels: **agents get expensive in their history, not their prompt**. Cutting context to save money can backfire.
