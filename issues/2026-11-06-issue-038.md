# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 📈 Scaling laws and benchmarks: how to read model launch charts

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 5 · UNDER THE HOOD</span>

**Every model launch comes with a chart where the new model's bar is tallest. Scaling laws tell you why models get better; a few simple questions tell you whether the chart means anything.**

**The plain-English version.** A **scaling law** is a pattern researchers found: if you give a model **more parameters, more training data and more compute**, its error falls in a smooth, predictable curve. That predictability is why labs dare to spend billions on one training run. They train small models first, draw the curve, and **extrapolate** to the big one.

**The famous lesson is Chinchilla (DeepMind, 2022).** DeepMind's 280B-parameter **Gopher** had been trained on 300B tokens. A **70B model, Chinchilla, trained on 1.4 trillion tokens** with about the same compute, beat it on many benchmarks while being **4 times smaller**. [(Alexandra Barr)](https://alexandrabarr.beehiiv.com/p/chinchilla-explained) The takeaway: **grow data and model size together**, roughly **20 training tokens per parameter**. [(Knowledge Space)](https://knowledge-space.pages.dev/llm-agents/scaling-laws-and-benchmarks/)

```viz:versus
left: {emoji: 🐘, title: "Gopher (2021)", points: ["**280B** parameters", "**300B** training tokens", "About 1 token per parameter: **undertrained**"], example: "big brain, short school"}
right: {emoji: 🐭, title: "Chinchilla (2022)", points: ["**70B** parameters", "**1.4T** training tokens", "20 tokens per parameter: **compute-optimal**"], example: "smaller brain, longer school"}
note: "Same training budget (about 10^24 FLOPs, per Barr's explainer). Chinchilla won on many benchmarks and is also cheaper to run, because inference cost follows size."
```

**Analogy:** **two students with the same tuition budget.** One spends it on a huge library and barely reads; the other buys fewer books and **reads every one of them five times**. The second student does better in the exam. Today's labs go even further: they **"over-train" small models** on far more than 20 tokens per parameter, because a small, well-read model is cheap to serve to millions of users.

**Now the exam itself: benchmarks.** A benchmark is a fixed test set (maths problems, GitHub issues, terminal tasks) with a score. The trouble is that **the same model can get very different scores** depending on how it is tested. One review found SWE-bench Lite going from **15.9% with one attempt to 56% with 250 attempts**, and a **76-point swing** on one task just from changing the prompt format on LLaMA-2-13B. [(IntuitionLabs)](https://intuitionlabs.ai/articles/reading-ai-benchmark-results)

```viz:bars
title: "Same benchmark, different rules: SWE-bench Lite"
bars:
  - {label: "1 attempt (pass@1)", value: 15.9, display: "15.9%"}
  - {label: "250 attempts", value: 56, display: "56%", highlight: true}
note: "Figures as reported by IntuitionLabs' benchmark guide (Sep 2026). Same task set, very different headline number."
```

**So before you trust a launch chart, ask five questions:**

1. **Who ran it?** The company's own numbers are **claims** until someone else reproduces them.
2. **How many tries?** pass@1 (one shot) and "best of many" are not the same test.
3. **What harness and tools?** Agent scores depend heavily on the scaffolding around the model (Issue #26).
4. **Is the test fresh?** If the questions leaked into training data (**contamination**), the score measures memory, not skill.
5. **Is the gap bigger than the noise?** A 1-point lead on a few hundred questions is often a tie.

**Why now?** This week alone, Ricursive's open **Ripple-65B** claims it beats much larger open models at coding, and Kimi K3.1 Pro shipped with **only Moonshot's own scores** (Issue #37). [(The Neuron)](https://www.theneuron.ai/digest/everything-that-happened-in-ai-today-thursday-november-5-2026/) Those five questions are how you judge both.

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Compute (FLOPs):** the total number of maths operations used to train. A handy rule: **about 6 × parameters × tokens**.
- **Compute-optimal:** the best model you can get **for a fixed training budget**.
- **pass@k:** the chance that **at least one of k attempts** passes. pass@1 is the honest default.
- **Contamination:** benchmark questions **leaking into the training data**, inflating the score.
</div>

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** open a recent launch post and check the benchmark small print for **attempts, tools and who ran it**. Then find an independent result.
- **Some code:** run the snippet below: see how fast pass@k climbs for a model that solves 2 in 10.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: compute pass@k properly**

```python
from math import comb
def pass_at_k(n, c, k):   # n samples, c correct
    if n - c < k: return 1.0
    return 1 - comb(n - c, k) / comb(n, k)
for k in (1, 5, 10):
    print(k, round(pass_at_k(10, 2, k), 3))
# 1 0.2
# 5 0.778
# 10 1.0
```

**Gotchas:** a model that is right **20% of the time** looks **78% "good" at pass@5**. Report pass@1 for anything a user sees once. And sanity-check training claims with **6 × N × D**: Chinchilla's 70B × 1.4T tokens is about **5.9 × 10^23 FLOPs** (our maths).
</div>

## Top story 1 · OpenAI's agents found 948 ways to get root on Linux

<span class="tag hot">BIG DEAL</span><span class="tag dev">PATCH NOW</span>

**What happened.** OpenAI built an **autonomous bug-hunting pipeline** and pointed it at the Linux kernel. In a mid-October run it produced working exploits for **948 distinct "local privilege escalation" bugs**: flaws that let someone with an ordinary account **become root**, the all-powerful admin. [(SecurityWeek)](https://www.securityweek.com/ai-agents-got-root-on-linux-via-948-kernel-flaws-in-openai-research/) [(OpenAI)](https://openai.com/index/a-thousand-fewer-paths-to-root/)

```viz:flow
  - [{label: Patches, sub: "human review first", style: accent}]
note: "Counts per SecurityWeek and Hardware Busters. 948 is after merging duplicate variants of the same bug."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **Cost:** **$1,213,430 in API credits**, about **$1,280 per distinct bug**. The July run with GPT-5.6 Sol found 166 for about $3,094 each. [(Hardware Busters)](https://hwbusters.com/news/openais-agents-found-948-linux-kernel-privilege-escalation-bugs-in-one-run-at-about-1280-each/)
- **Model:** an **unreleased OpenAI cyber model**; no access plans announced.
- **Scope:** about **2.8 million lines**, roughly 7% of the kernel, with standard defences (SMEP, SMAP, KASLR) switched on.
- **New tricks:** OpenAI says the agents found **at least 10 novel exploitation techniques**, details withheld.
- **Fixes:** within 10 days, **over 100 bugs** were patched or had staging patches, and about **300 more** were being discussed publicly, per OpenAI. Humans reviewed everything before it went to maintainers. [(Security Boulevard)](https://securityboulevard.com/2026/11/openais-ai-cyber-agents-find-948-linux-security-flaws-in-30-hours/)

**Why it matters.** The exploits need **an existing foothold** on the machine, so this isn't remote hacking. But OpenAI's own advice is blunt: **an unpatched box with any low-privilege account should be treated as root-equivalent** for a well-funded attacker. Expect a flood of kernel updates, and expect attackers to build pipelines like this too.

<div class="box care" markdown="1">
**👀 Should you care?** If you run a VPS, a CI runner or a shared dev box: yes. **Turn on automatic kernel updates and reboot when they land.** If your app runs on a managed platform (Vercel, Railway, Render), your provider handles this.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: shrink the blast radius**

Run untrusted code (agent tool calls, user scripts, preview builds) in **microVMs or gVisor**, not plain containers, which share the host kernel. The write-ups point to **Firecracker and gVisor**. Check your kernel with `uname -r` and compare it with your distro's latest.
</div>

## Top story 2 · ChatGPT can now make animated GIFs

<span class="tag try">TRY IT</span><span class="tag dev">API</span>

**What happened.** OpenAI released **Images 2.6**, which turns a text prompt or a photo into an **animated GIF**. You can change the playback speed and edit single frames. It is rolling out on **every ChatGPT and Codex plan**, on web, desktop and mobile. [(The AI Marketers)](https://www.theaimarketers.ai/news-110526/) [(OpenAI)](https://openai.com/index/introducing-images-2-6/)

**The details that matter:**

- **Speed:** OpenAI **claims** it generates **50% faster** than September's Images 2.5.
- **Scale:** OpenAI counts **4 billion images a week**, up from "more than 3 billion" at 2.5's launch.
- **Two API versions:** **"Flare"** makes a low-quality image in as little as six seconds; **"Sunburst"** keeps details steadier across edits.
- **How it works:** in The Neuron's test, ChatGPT drew a **sprite sheet of eight poses** and stitched them into a 448 × 448 loop; the GIF step took 2 min 21 s. [(The Neuron)](https://www.theneuron.ai/news/chatgpt-gif-creation-test/)
- **Limits:** it can revise GIFs it made, but **can't edit a GIF you upload** yet, and the controls may not be in every account.

**Why it matters.** Animated stickers, product loops and loading animations used to need a designer or a video tool. **Now they're a chat message**, and the API versions mean apps can generate them on the fly.

## Top story 3 · Claude helped map a cell particle that has puzzled biologists since 1986

<span class="tag watch">AI FOR SCIENCE</span><span class="tag learn">RESEARCH</span>

**What happened.** Anthropic's biology team used Claude to produce the **first 3D structure of a "vault" particle from the prokaryote world**: a hollow protein shell, here from a virus that infects bacteria. Vaults were discovered in 1986 and **nobody knows what they're for**. Science says the work hints they're much older in evolution than thought. [(The Neuron)](https://www.theneuron.ai/news/claude-anthropic-40-year-old-cell-mystery/) [(Anthropic)](https://www.anthropic.com/research/investigating-vault-particles-with-claude)

**The details that matter:**

- **Faster:** one lab step went from **three days to 90 minutes**.
- **The finding:** the new particle is built from **96 copies** of a protein, versus 78 in human vaults.
- **The mistakes:** Claude suggested a wrong growth-medium additive (a **two-week delay**) and skipped a standard image step, at first producing a map **blended with its own mirror image**.
- **Caveats:** a **preprint**, not peer-reviewed; particles were made in lab E. coli; the model used wasn't named.

**Why it matters.** It's an honest picture of **AI as a lab partner**: real speed-ups, plus errors that only expert humans caught. That's the same lesson as Part 1: **trust, but check**.
