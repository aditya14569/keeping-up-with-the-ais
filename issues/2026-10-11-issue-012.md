# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 🎛️ Picking the right model for the job

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 2 · GETTING GREAT RESULTS</span>

**Every AI lab now sells a family of models, from tiny and cheap to huge and slow. Picking well is mostly about matching the model to the task, not finding "the best" one.**

Every model choice trades off three things: **quality** (does it get the answer right?), **speed** (how long until the first word, and the last?) and **cost** (what you pay per million tokens). You can rarely max out all three. **Bigger models are usually smarter, but slower and pricier.**

**Analogy:** you don't book a flight from Andheri to Bandra. An auto gets you there faster and for a fraction of the price. For Mumbai to Delhi, though, the flight wins easily. **The right vehicle depends on the trip.** Same with models: a tiny model can sort support tickets all day, while a frontier model earns its price on a gnarly refactor.

```viz:versus
title: Two sensible ways to start
left:
  emoji: 🛵
  title: Efficiency-first
  points:
    - Start with the **smallest, cheapest** model
    - Test it on your real tasks
    - Upgrade only where it **visibly fails**
  example: "Good for: prototypes, high volume, tight latency"
right:
  emoji: 🚀
  title: Capability-first
  points:
    - Start with a **frontier** model
    - Get the quality right first
    - Then **lower effort or step down** to cut cost
  example: "Good for: hard reasoning, coding agents"
note: "Both approaches are from Anthropic's 'Choosing a model' guide. Either way, the step that matters is testing on your own prompts and data."
```

**The second dial: effort.** Many models now have a reasoning "effort" setting (low, medium, high and so on). Anthropic's docs say **tuning effort is often a better lever than switching models**. OpenAI's guide says something similar: compare settings on the same inputs and keep **"the lightest setting that meets your quality bar."** [(Claude Docs)](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/choosing-a-model) [(OpenAI)](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/model-selection)

**The third option: use more than one.** Real apps often route each request to the cheapest model that can handle it, so most tokens bill at the low rate:

```viz:flow
title: A simple model router
headers: [Request, Router, Model, Answer]
columns:
  - - {label: User message, sub: any task}
  - - {label: Decision model, sub: "easy or hard?", style: accent}
  - - {label: Small model, sub: most traffic, style: soft}
    - {label: Frontier model, sub: hard cases only, style: warn}
  - - {label: Reply, sub: logged + scored}
note: "Illustrative. Anthropic's docs describe two versions: a cheap 'executor' that escalates hard calls to an 'advisor', and an 'orchestrator' that hands bulk work to cheaper 'workers'."
```

**Why now?** Model families keep splitting into more tiers, plus a new class of **decision models** that only pick answers (see Part 2). On OpenRouter's usage rankings, cheap "Flash" models currently sit at the top by tokens processed. **There, at least, most traffic isn't going to the flagships.** [(OpenRouter)](https://openrouter.ai/rankings?view=day)

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Tier:** a model's size class in its family (think Haiku / Sonnet / Opus, or Luna / Sol / Astra).
- **Latency:** how long you wait. "Time to first token" matters most for chat.
- **Eval:** a fixed set of your own test cases with known good answers, used to compare models fairly.
- **Router:** code (or a small model) that decides which model handles each request.
</div>

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** take one task you do daily (say, summarising a long email). Run it through a **small and a large model** from the same family, side by side. Would you notice the difference? If not, the small one wins.
- **Some code:** write down **10 real inputs** and what a good answer looks like. Run the script below against two models. That's your first eval.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: a 15-line model bake-off**

```python
import time
from anthropic import Anthropic
client = Anthropic()

cases = [("Refund not received after 7 days", "billing"),
         ("App crashes on login", "bug")]  # add ~20 real ones

for model in ["claude-haiku-5-5", "claude-sonnet-5-5"]:
    right, t0 = 0, time.time()
    for text, want in cases:
        r = client.messages.create(model=model, max_tokens=5,
            messages=[{"role": "user", "content":
              f"Label as billing, bug or other. One word.\n\n{text}"}])
        right += r.content[0].text.strip().lower() == want
    print(model, f"{right}/{len(cases)}", f"{time.time()-t0:.1f}s")
```

**Gotchas:** public benchmarks measure *someone else's* task, so **your eval beats any leaderboard**. Track cost per *task*, not per token: a cheaper model that needs three retries isn't cheaper. And re-run your eval when a provider ships a new version, because **the ranking can flip**.
</div>

## Top story 1 · Microsoft's Decision-1: an AI that picks, not chats

<span class="tag hot">BIG DEAL</span><span class="tag dev">DECISION MODELS</span><span class="tag try">TRY IT</span>

**What happened.** Microsoft released **Microsoft-Decision-1**, a model for routing, classification, checking and agent guardrails. You give it a question and a fixed set of options; it returns **a calibrated probability for each option**, as JSON, in one call. No essay. [(MarkTechPost)](https://www.marktechpost.com/2026/10/09/microsoft-ai-releases-microsoft-decision-1-a-qwen3-5-9b-decision-scoring-model/)

```viz:bars

```

**The details that matter:**

- **Price:** **$0.042 per million input tokens, and output is free.** OpenAI's equivalent Decisions endpoint charges $0.10 per million input. [(OpenRouter)](https://openrouter.ai/microsoft/microsoft-decision-1) [(Let's Data Science)](https://letsdatascience.com/news/openai-opens-decisions-api-public-beta-b69a1e40)
- **Under the hood:** it's **post-trained from Alibaba's open Qwen3.5-9B**, text-only, with a 32K-token context. Weights are **not** released; it's API-only via Microsoft Foundry and OpenRouter.
- **Microsoft claims** 83.5% accuracy across 36 benchmarks and 147,137 questions, ahead of GPT-6 Luna Decisions at 79.4%. Its answers flipped on **only 1.3%** of reworded or shuffled questions. **All vendor-run**; independent JevBench results are pending.
- **Not for:** chat, summaries, translation, or being the **sole decider** on credit, jobs, housing, health or legal matters.

**Why it matters.** When Microsoft, OpenAI, Cloudflare, Perplexity and a $7.5B startup all ship the same kind of product within a month, it's a category. **The plumbing of AI apps (routing, tagging, "is this safe?") is moving off chat models** onto cheaper, faster specialists.

## Top story 2 · Cloudflare buys Deno, with a deadline

<span class="tag dev">DEV TOOLS</span><span class="tag watch">ACQUISITION</span>

**What happened.** Cloudflare is acquiring **Deno**, the company behind the JavaScript/TypeScript runtime created by Node.js inventor **Ryan Dahl**. The whole team joins Cloudflare to work on **Workers**, its serverless platform. Terms weren't disclosed. [(BetaNews)](https://betanews.com/article/cloudflare-has-acquired-deno-and-will-use-it-to-boost-workers/)

```viz:flow
note: "From Deno's announcement as quoted by BetaNews and DEV Community. The runtime stays open source, so the community could fork it."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **Deno runtime:** monthly bug-fix and security releases **for one more year**, then official development ends. It stays open source.
- **Deno Deploy:** runs for **about six months**, then shuts down. Paying customers get help moving to Workers.
- **JSR**, the JavaScript package registry, keeps running, with its servers moving to Cloudflare.
- **The real plan:** merge **celld**, Deno's self-hostable take on Workers, into **workerd**, Cloudflare's open-source Workers runtime, so you can run Workers-style apps **on your own servers**.

**Why it matters.** Lots of AI demos and agent backends run on edge runtimes like Deno Deploy and Workers. One option just disappeared, and **"run Workers anywhere" just got a big push.**

## Top story 3 · AI answers make people quit sooner

<span class="tag watch">RESEARCH</span><span class="tag learn">VIBE CODERS</span>

**What happened.** A UC Berkeley-led team ran three randomised experiments with **1,222 people** online. One group could ask ChatGPT for help on fraction problems or SAT-style reading questions; then, after about 10 minutes, **the AI was taken away**. Their accuracy dropped, and they **skipped more questions** than people who never had AI. [(EurekAlert)](https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1147258)

```viz:bars
title: "Solve rate after the AI was removed"
bars:
  - {label: Fractions · no AI, value: 73, display: "73%"}
  - {label: Fractions · had AI, value: 57, display: "57%", highlight: true}
  - {label: Reading · no AI, value: 89, display: "89%"}
  - {label: Reading · had AI, value: 76, display: "76%", highlight: true}
note: "Experiments 1 (n=354) and 3 (n=201), from the paper's project page. Skip rates rose too: 11% to 20% on fractions, 1% to 8% on reading."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **It's how you use it.** In the replication, **61% of AI users asked for direct answers**, and they showed the biggest drops. People who asked for **hints or clarifications showed no significant harm**. [(Project page)](https://ai-project-website.github.io/AI-assistance-reduces-persistence/)
- **Peer-reviewed** at COLM, with co-authors from CMU, MIT, Oxford and UCLA. **Limits:** short tasks, one session. Lead author Brian Christian wants AI to act **more like a tutor**; long-term effects **weren't tested**.

**Why it matters.** Vibe coding is built on asking for answers. This doesn't say "stop", but it hints that **how you prompt shapes what you can still do on your own**.
