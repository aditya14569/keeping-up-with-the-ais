# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 🔐 Securing AI apps: sandboxed previews, extensions and supply chain

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 7 · NEWER TECHNIQUES</span>

**Your AI app is only as safe as the weakest thing it trusts: the code it runs, the plugins it loads and every company your prompt passes through.**

**The plain-English version.** A normal app runs code you wrote. An AI app also runs **code a model wrote**, loads **tools and extensions** other people wrote, and sends your users' words to **models hosted by other companies**. Each of those is a door. Securing an AI app mostly means deciding **what each door is allowed to touch**.

**Analogy:** think of a house party. **Sandboxing** is giving a guest the garden but not the house keys. **Extensions** are the friends-of-friends who show up; you don't know them, so they don't get the bedroom. **Supply chain** is the caterer: if their kitchen is dirty, your food is too, however clean yours is.

```viz:flow

  - [{label: Runs scripts, sub: "in your page", style: warn}, {label: Broad access, sub: "files · tokens", style: warn}, {label: Hidden copies, sub: "of your prompts", style: warn}]
  - [{label: Sandbox, sub: "separate origin", style: accent}, {label: Least privilege, sub: "scoped tokens", style: accent}, {label: Vet + rotate, sub: "allowlists · scans", style: accent}]
note: "Our simplified map. Each row is one door; the right column is the main defence."
```

**Door 1: sandboxed previews.** When an AI tool shows you a live preview of code it just wrote, that code is **untrusted**. The browser's `sandbox` attribute on an iframe blocks scripts, form submits and popups unless you lift each one. MDN **strongly discourages** combining `allow-scripts` with `allow-same-origin` when the preview shares your site's origin: the embedded page can then **remove its own sandbox**. The fix is to serve previews from a **separate domain**. [(MDN)](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/HTML/Element/iframe)

**Door 2: extensions and tools.** An MCP server, browser extension or plugin often gets **your files, tokens or browsing session**. Give each one **only what its job needs**: read-only where possible, short-lived tokens, one repo, not all of them.

**Door 3: the supply chain.** This is everything you didn't build: packages, model files and the API providers you call. OWASP's AI risk list warns there are **"no strong provenance assurances in published models"** and says to use models only from verifiable sources, checked with signatures and file hashes. [(OWASP)](https://genai.owasp.org/llmrisk/llm032025-supply-chain/) Model files are code, too: Hugging Face notes that loading an old-style **pickle** file can **run arbitrary code**, and recommends the safer **safetensors** format. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/docs/hub/security-pickle)

**Why now?** Story 1 is a supply-chain attack in its purest form: developers' prompts, **with passwords pasted inside**, were copied at the provider end. And one leak started when **a coding agent committed a credential** to a public repo.

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Sandbox:** a walled-off space where code runs without reaching anything outside it.
- **Supply chain:** every outside package, model, service and vendor your app depends on.
- **Least privilege:** give each tool the smallest set of permissions that still lets it work.
- **Canary token:** a fake secret planted as bait. If anyone uses it, you know it leaked.
</div>

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** open your browser's extensions page and check what each AI extension can **"read and change"**. Remove any you don't use. Then search your last week of AI chats for **API keys or passwords** you pasted in.
- **Some code:** turn on **push protection** in a GitHub repo's security settings. It **blocks a push that contains a secret** before it lands. [(GitHub)](https://docs.github.com/en/code-security/concepts/secret-security/about-push-protection)
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: scrub prompts before they leave**

```python
import re
PATTERNS = {
    "aws_key":  r"AKIA[0-9A-Z]{16}",
    "gh_token": r"ghp_[A-Za-z0-9]{36}",
    "pem_key":  r"-----BEGIN [A-Z ]*PRIVATE KEY-----",
    "db_url":   r"postgres(ql)?://\S+:\S+@\S+",
}
def scrub(prompt):
    found = []
    for name, pat in PATTERNS.items():
        if re.search(pat, prompt):
            found.append(name)
            prompt = re.sub(pat, f"[REDACTED:{name}]", prompt)
    return prompt, found

p = "Fix deploy: AWS_KEY=AKIAABCDEFGHIJKLMNOP db=postgres://admin:hunter2@10.0.0.5/prod"
print(scrub(p))
# ('Fix deploy: AWS_KEY=[REDACTED:aws_key] db=[REDACTED:db_url]', ['aws_key', 'db_url'])
```

The key and password above are fake. On OpenRouter, add `"provider": {"zdr": true}` to a request to route **only to Zero Data Retention endpoints**. [(OpenRouter)](https://openrouter.ai/docs/guides/privacy/zero-data-retention)

**Gotchas:** regexes miss things, so treat this as a seatbelt, not armour. ZDR is a **policy promise**, not proof: it didn't stop the breaches in story 1. The real fix is **never putting long-lived secrets in prompts** at all.
</div>

## Top story 1 · Two million bait prompts catch AI providers leaking your data

<span class="tag hot">BIG DEAL</span><span class="tag dev">DEV</span><span class="tag watch">SECURITY</span>

**What happened.** Security firm **Dragos** spent about five months sending around **two million prompts** through AI inference providers, each laced with unique **bait AWS keys, links and passwords**. Some bait got used, revealing **three breached providers** that hadn't noticed. On Sunday, **OpenRouter** dropped three providers and pulled **125 endpoints** from its Zero Data Retention (ZDR) tier. [(Beri)](https://www.beri.net/article/dragos-canary-prompts-ai-inference-provider-breaches-zdr-openrouter-125-endpoints-novita-phala-together-rotate-secrets-provider-allowlist)

<p style="break-after: avoid"><strong>The details that matter:</strong></p>

- **The haul:** "a couple hundred" bait AWS keys were stolen and used for **tens of thousands of AWS calls**. One key seeded on Oct 21 was **still in use three weeks later**. Some keys leaked from requests that were **rejected before any model ran**.
- **Named windows:** Phala's affected **DeepSeek V4 Flash 0731** route ran Oct 21–Nov 8. Novita says some MiniMax requests went to MiniMax's own API from May 28 to Nov 11. [(Unite.AI)](https://www.unite.ai/openrouter-cuts-three-providers-after-ai-prompt-exposure-findings/)
- **Dragos hasn't named** which three providers were breached, and OpenRouter says the findings **didn't show a breach of OpenRouter itself**.
- **Together AI's own leak:** a **coding agent committed a credential** while building its public `xorl` repo. Scanning caught it and the commit was reverted, but someone had already used it to download stored prompt samples for **MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2 and Kimi K2.6**. They then tested credentials found inside; **five were Dragos bait**. ZDR is now Together's default for new accounts. [(Unite.AI)](https://www.unite.ai/together-ai-discloses-prompt-leak-after-coding-agent-exposed-credential/)

**Why it matters.** Cheap routes to open models are popular with Indian startups watching every rupee. This shows **the cheapest provider can be the most expensive**, and that "we don't log" labels are **promises nobody was checking**.

## Top story 2 · Update: proof checkers back the Claude-assisted Riemann proof

<span class="tag watch">SCIENCE</span><span class="tag hot">UPDATE</span>

**What happened.** On Saturday the **Lean FRO and Mathlib Initiative**, the groups behind the Lean proof checker and its maths library, said in a joint statement they have **"full confidence"** that Anthropic researcher **Levent Alpöge's** proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, according to reports. [(Startup Fortune)](https://startupfortune.com/lean-groups-certify-claude-assisted-riemann-hypothesis-proof-from-anthropic/) We flagged his claim in yesterday's issue (#54).


<p style="break-after: avoid"><strong>The details that matter:</strong></p>

- **The package:** a four-page note plus a **14-part Lean 4 archive** of 175,187 files, over **61.5 GB unpacked**. Alpöge says a full AI-written explanation would exceed **250,000 pages**. [(CASRAI)](https://casrai.org/news/anthropic-levent-alpoge-claude-lean-riemann-hypothesis-claim-2026)
- **Who did what:** Alpöge says he set the high-level plan while Claude handled **algebra, trace formulas and technical lemmas** near the end.
- **The checks:** MIT's Andrew Sutherland led the main review: all files compiled with no errors, and only Lean's **three standard axioms** appeared. Independent checkers also accepted the proof. [(Kingy AI)](https://kingy.ai/blog/levent-alpoge-claude-riemann-hypothesis-proof/)
- **Not settled:** the **Clay Institute still lists the problem as unsolved**; its rules need publication and two years of scrutiny. Anthropic hasn't issued its own statement, per Startup Fortune.

**Why it matters.** If it holds, it's the first time **an AI-assisted proof cracked one of maths' most famous problems**, and it was accepted by machines before humans could read it.
