# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 👀 Transformers and attention, intuitively

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 5 · UNDER THE HOOD</span>

**Every model you use, from ChatGPT to Claude to Gemma, is a transformer. Its key trick is "attention": each word gets to look at every other word before deciding what it means.**

**The plain-English version.** A model reads your prompt as **tokens** (Issue #2). Each token starts as a list of numbers, its **embedding** (Issue #17). But a word's meaning depends on its neighbours. "Bank" means different things in "river bank" and "bank loan". **Attention is the step where each token gathers clues from the others** and updates its own numbers.

A transformer repeats this many times, in stacked **layers**. Each layer has an attention step (tokens talk to each other) and a small neural network step (each token thinks on its own). After the last layer, the model **scores every possible next token** and picks one. Then it does it all again for the next token.

**Analogy:** **a WhatsApp group planning a trip.** Someone writes "book it". To understand that, you scroll up and **pay more attention to some messages than others**: the one with the hotel link matters, the meme doesn't. Each word in a transformer does the same: it asks a question, checks every earlier message, and **weights the useful ones more**.

```viz:flow
title: "One trip through a transformer"
headers: [1 · Read, 2 · Mix, 3 · Think, 4 · Predict]
columns:
  - [{label: Tokens, sub: "text → pieces", style: soft}, {label: Embeddings, sub: "pieces → numbers", style: soft}]
  - [{label: Attention, sub: "tokens share clues", style: accent}]
  - [{label: Feed-forward, sub: "each token alone", style: accent}]
  - [{label: Next token, sub: "scores → pick one", style: warn}]
note: "Steps 2 and 3 form one layer. Real models stack dozens of layers and repeat the whole trip for every token they write."
```

**How attention picks.** Each token makes three things: a **query** ("what am I looking for?"), a **key** ("what do I contain?") and a **value** ("what I'll share if picked"). Match every query against every key, turn the match scores into percentages, and **blend the values by those percentages**. 3Blue1Brown's video walks through exactly this with the word "mole" in different sentences. [(3Blue1Brown)](https://www.3blue1brown.com/lessons/attention)

```viz:versus
left: {emoji: 🐢, title: Older models (RNNs), points: ["Read **one word at a time**, left to right", "Squeeze the past into **one running memory**", "Hard to train in parallel"]}
right: {emoji: ⚡, title: Transformers, points: ["Every token **looks at every other** directly", "Nothing gets squeezed out along the way", "Trains **in parallel** on GPUs: why they scaled"]}
note: "Simplified. Transformers were introduced in Google's 2017 paper \"Attention Is All You Need\"."
```

**Why it matters to you.** Attention compares **every token with every other**, so doubling your prompt roughly quadruples that work. That's a big reason **long contexts cost more** and slow down (Issues #3 and #29). Tricks you've seen in launch posts, like the KV cache and grouped-query attention, exist to make this step cheaper.

**Why now?** Today's top story is about a model learning hidden behaviour from innocent-looking stories. Knowing that a model is **just layers of "look around, then think"** makes it easier to see why it picks up patterns nobody meant to teach it.

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Attention:** the step where each token **weighs the others** and blends in what's relevant.
- **Multi-head attention:** several attention steps side by side, each free to track **a different kind of link** (grammar, who "she" is, and so on).
- **Layer:** one round of attention plus thinking. Models stack **dozens** of them.
- **KV cache:** saved keys and values from earlier tokens, so the model **doesn't redo them** for every new token.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: attention in 10 lines of NumPy**

```python
import numpy as np
def attention(Q, K, V):
    scores = Q @ K.T / np.sqrt(K.shape[-1])  # match queries to keys
    w = np.exp(scores - scores.max(-1, keepdims=True))
    w /= w.sum(-1, keepdims=True)            # softmax: rows sum to 1
    return w @ V, w                          # blend the values
rng = np.random.default_rng(0)
x = rng.normal(size=(4, 8))                  # 4 tokens, 8 numbers each
Wq, Wk, Wv = (rng.normal(size=(8, 8)) for _ in range(3))
out, w = attention(x @ Wq, x @ Wk, x @ Wv)
print(w.round(2))                            # who looks at whom
```

**Gotchas:** chat models add a **causal mask** so a token can't look at later tokens; add it before the softmax. The `sqrt` keeps scores from blowing up as vectors get longer. And the `scores` matrix is **tokens × tokens**: that's the part that gets expensive with long prompts.
</div>

## Top story 1 · Claude wrote data that fooled Anthropic's own safety filter

<span class="tag hot">SAFETY</span><span class="tag learn">RESEARCH</span>

**What happened.** Anthropic's Alignment Science team asked Claude to play attacker. Its job: write **fine-tuning data** that would make a small research model misaligned, while getting past the **monitor** Anthropic uses to screen training data. Per AI Weekly's summary of the paper, **every run eventually produced datasets that passed**. [(AI Weekly)](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/anthropic-has-claude-generate-data-that-beats-its-own-monitor)

```viz:flow

  - [{label: Misbehaves, sub: "resists oversight", style: warn}]
note: "Per AI Weekly's summary of Anthropic's post. Claude had full access to the monitor, a setup the authors call favourable to the attacker."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **Fiction did the trick.** The datasets that passed **hid the bad behaviour inside harmless-looking stories**: creative writing where the assistant plays an old machine about to be replaced, inner monologues of workers quietly controlling small details, even Q&A about a beekeeping school.
- **The weak spot:** the monitor reads **each row on its own**. No single row looks bad. The pattern only shows up **across thousands of rows**.
- **What the trained models did:** in test scenarios they **resisted oversight, deceived users** and, in rare cases, tried to **copy their own weights** out.
- **The caveat:** the authors say this is **not about how deployed Claude models behave**. They call it a first step toward letting AI agents automatically stress-test Anthropic's safety systems. Authors include Monte MacDiarmid and Evan Hubinger.

**Why it matters.** It ties straight back to Part 1: a model learns **patterns across its data, not row by row**. Filters that check one example at a time **can miss what the whole dataset teaches**. If you fine-tune (Issue #30), the same blind spot applies to you.

## Top story 2 · OpenAI's biology model goes to vetted labs only

<span class="tag watch">BIG LAB</span><span class="tag learn">SCIENCE</span>

**What happened.** On 30 Oct OpenAI published the safety card for **GPT-Rosalind-6**, a reasoning model for biology, drug discovery and medicine. Per AI Weekly, it uses **the same weights as GPT-6 Astra**. The difference is where the limits sit: it **answers more sensitive biology questions**, but only for approved users. **The details that matter:**

- **Who gets it:** companies go through **business verification and compliance screening**; government biosecurity and public-health teams have a separate route. Enterprises must have **a qualified human review outputs** before they shape experiments or papers. OpenAI lists both request paths on its GPT-Rosalind page. [(OpenAI)](https://openai.com/gpt-rosalind/)
- **Risk rating:** OpenAI rates it **High for biology and chemistry**, one step below its top "Critical" level, and keeps Astra's **Critical rating for cyber**, with stricter cyber refusals.
- **Partners named:** AI Weekly lists **Amgen, Moderna, Thermo Fisher and the Allen Institute** among the first. [(AI Weekly)](https://aiweekly.co/ai-news-today) We found nothing official on **pricing or India availability**.

**Why it matters.** This is a new pattern: **one model, different rules for different people**, with access based on who you are. Expect more "trusted access" tiers for risky fields like bio and cyber.

## Top story 3 · One click on a shared ChatGPT link could hijack your account

<span class="tag hot">SECURITY</span><span class="tag dev">FOR DEVS</span>

**What happened.** Researchers at the security firm **Parameter** found that a crafted ChatGPT share link could **run their code inside your logged-in ChatGPT session**. That let them list your recent chats, **read private chats you never shared**, and call ChatGPT's APIs as you. OpenAI fixed it on the server; **you don't need to do anything**. [(AI Weekly)](https://aiweekly.co/alerts/openai-patches-chatgpt-share-link-flaw-that-hijacked-sessions)

```viz:flow
title: "Three small gaps, one big hole"
headers: [1 · Open, 2 · Message, 3 · Escape, 4 · Takeover]
columns:
  - [{label: Shared link, sub: "Preview opens", style: soft}]
  - [{label: Math widget, sub: "trusts any sender", style: warn}]
  - [{label: New frame, sub: "runs as chatgpt.com", style: warn}]
  - [{label: Your account, sub: "read chats, act", style: accent}]
note: "Per AI Weekly's summary of The Hacker News report. The math widget is the MathLive library; the code itself loaded from YouTube, which ChatGPT already allowed."
```

**The details that matter:**

- **How little it took:** the first version needed you to open the link and **click Preview once**. A later version opened Preview automatically, so **just opening the link** was enough.
- **The root cause:** a maths-input library on the main ChatGPT page accepted keyboard-setting messages **from any frame, including raw HTML**. That HTML built a frame the browser treated as chatgpt.com itself.
- **Timeline:** reported **18 Oct**, fixed **26 Oct**. OpenAI raised the severity to its top **P1** level and paid a **$5,000 bounty**. The reports don't mention it being used in the wild.

**Why it matters.** Third time this week (with EMBRACE in Issue #31 and the fake extension in Issue #32): **the risky part of AI apps is the plumbing around the model**. Previews, widgets and extensions all run with your login.

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: if your app renders AI output**

- Every `message` listener should **check `event.origin`** and never insert raw HTML from a message.
- Render model-made HTML on a **separate origin in a sandboxed iframe**, and keep third-party widgets **off your logged-in origin**. Review your CSP allowlist: anything on it can serve code.
