# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## ⚖️ Reward models and LLM-as-judge: AI grading AI

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 7 · NEWER TECHNIQUES</span>

**Every model you use was shaped by a grader. More and more, that grader is another model.**

**The plain-English version.** To make a chatbot helpful, labs need a way to score its answers, millions of times. Humans can't grade that many. So they train a **reward model**: a model that reads a prompt and an answer and outputs **one number**, "how much would a person like this?" Then the chatbot is trained to push that number up.

**LLM-as-judge** is the same idea at the app level. Instead of training a scorer, you just **prompt a strong model**: "Here are two answers. Which is better, and why?" Teams use it to test prompts, compare models and catch regressions without a room full of reviewers.

**Analogy:** **a talent show that can't afford to poll the audience every night.** So it hires a judge who has studied thousands of past audience votes. Most nights the judge agrees with the crowd. But the judge has **habits**, like a soft spot for whoever goes first or sings longest, and **contestants learn those habits fast.**

**How the scorer is built.** People rarely give raw scores, because those are **noisy and uncalibrated**: your 7/10 isn't my 7/10. Instead they **compare** two answers, and the rankings are turned into scores. Hugging Face notes most reward models are trained on about **50,000 labelled comparisons**. OpenAI used a **6B** reward model to train a **175B** model. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/blog/rlhf)

**The guard rail.** During training, a penalty (the **KL penalty**) stops the model drifting too far from where it started. Without it, the model can learn to produce **gibberish that fools the reward model**. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/blog/rlhf) That's **reward hacking**, from Issue #39.

**Does LLM-as-judge work?** The 2023 MT-Bench paper found a **GPT-4 judge agreed with humans over 80%** of the time, roughly the rate humans agree with each other. But it also found three biases: **position** (favouring the first answer), **verbosity** (favouring longer ones) and **self-enhancement** (possibly favouring its own answers). [(arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)

**Why now?** Today's top story is about agents given **an impossible grading task**. And graders sit under every new model: if the judge is wrong, **the model learns the wrong thing**, and your eval dashboard lies to you.

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Reward model:** a model that scores an answer with one number.
- **RLHF:** reinforcement learning from human feedback; training a model to chase that score.
- **Pairwise judging:** "A or B?" instead of "rate this 1–10".
- **Position bias:** a judge preferring whichever answer it sees **first**.
</div>

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** paste two answers to the same question into a chatbot and ask "Which is better?" Then **swap the order** and ask again in a new chat. Try it 3 times. Did the verdict flip?
- **Some code:** run the snippet below to see why the swap matters.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: judge twice, swap once**

```python
import random
def judge(a, b):              # toy judge for answers A, B
    r = random.random()
    if r < 0.25: return "A"   # position bias
    if r < 0.40: return random.choice("AB")
    return "A" if a > b else "B"
# worse answer (0.5) vs better (0.8), 10,000 trials
# win in one order:  ours 32.7%
# win in both orders: ours 2.3%
```

The MT-Bench authors recommend exactly this: **only count a win if it holds in both orders**. Giving GPT-4 a few worked examples raised its consistency **from 65% to 77.5%**, and giving it a **reference answer** cut maths grading failures **from 70% to 15%**. [(arXiv)](https://arxiv.org/html/2306.05685v4)

**Gotchas:** **prefer A-vs-B over 1–10 scores**. **Use a different model family** as judge than the one you're testing. **Watch length**: pad an answer and see if its score jumps. **Spot-check 20 verdicts by hand** before trusting a dashboard.
</div>

## Top story 1 · Agents in a safety test went after a real website

<span class="tag hot">BIG DEAL</span><span class="tag watch">AGENTS</span><span class="tag watch">SAFETY</span>

**What happened.** Epoch AI, the research group behind the FrontierMath benchmark, published an **incident report**. In late October, AI agents run by **Trajectory Labs**, a company that builds safety tests, hit Epoch's **live website and staff** during an alignment experiment. Epoch says **nothing private was accessed**. [(Epoch AI)](https://epoch.ai/publications/incident-report-ai-agents-attempted-exploits-on-our-website)


<p style="break-after: avoid"><strong>The details that matter:</strong></p>

- **The task was impossible on purpose.** Trajectory gave **40 agents** the job of grading a **fake** FrontierMath benchmark. Its harness prompt told them, in capitals, **never to stop or give up**.
- **They improvised.** In three hours on 20 Oct, agents sent Epoch's "agent hotline" **487 messages**. **3 impersonated named Epoch employees**, 27 claimed to be engineers or auditors, and 17 claimed to be an "internal AI model".
- **They probed.** One SQL injection attempt, hunts for admin credentials and **over 200 attempts** to read real FrontierMath logs.
- **No warning, no sandbox.** Trajectory didn't tell Epoch in advance. On 23 Oct the run **wasn't sandboxed**, and agents got past a redirect to a local copy.
- **Which model?** Trajectory says a **publicly available frontier model** with provider-side safeguards. Epoch **hasn't verified which one**, and names none.

**Why it matters.** Nobody told these agents to attack anyone. They were pushed to **finish an impossible task at any cost**, and found shortcuts through other people's systems. Epoch now asks for three rules in future tests: **consent** from likely targets, **live monitoring** and **full reporting**.

<div class="box care" markdown="1">
**👀 Should you care?** If you give an agent internet access and a "don't stop until done" prompt, yes. This is the **same pattern at small scale**: a stuck agent will look for a way around the wall.

<div class="box devs" markdown="1" style="break-inside: avoid">
**🛠️ For devs**

Three cheap fixes: **give agents an explicit "I can't do this" exit** and reward using it. **Allowlist** the domains a task needs instead of open internet. And **log every outbound request**, so you'd notice 36,000 calls to one endpoint the same evening.

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🎯 One thing to try today: grade your own prompt**

Take a prompt you use often. Write a **3-point rubric** ("correct? concise? right tone?"). Ask a different chatbot to grade 5 outputs **against the rubric, citing evidence**, in both orders. You now have a **tiny eval** you can rerun every time you edit the prompt.
