# Part 1 · Learn one thing

## 📱 On-device AI: running models on phones and laptops

<span class="tag learn">4 MIN READ</span><span class="tag learn">MODULE 7 · NEWER TECHNIQUES</span>

**Most AI you use lives in a data centre. On-device AI moves the model into your pocket, and the hard part is making it fit.**

**The plain-English version.** When you chat with a cloud AI, your words travel to a server, a huge model answers, and the reply travels back. **On-device AI** runs the model on your own phone or laptop instead. Nothing leaves the device, it **works in airplane mode**, and there's **no per-message bill**. The catch: your phone has a few gigabytes of memory, not hundreds.

**Analogy:** a cloud model is a **full library**; an on-device model is the **pocket dictionary** you carry on the train. It knows less and is a bit rougher, but it's there when the network isn't, and nobody sees what you looked up.

```viz:flow
title: "How a model gets onto your device"
headers: [Train, Shrink, Package, Run]
columns:
  - [{label: Full model, sub: "16-bit weights"}]
  - [{label: Quantise, sub: "16 → 8 or 4 bits", style: accent}, {label: Distil or prune, sub: "fewer parameters", style: soft}]
  - [{label: GGUF / MLX, sub: "laptops"}, {label: LiteRT / Core ML, sub: "phones"}]
  - [{label: CPU · GPU · NPU, sub: "on your device", style: accent}]
note: "Our simplified sketch. Formats named in the llama.cpp, LiteRT-LM and Apple docs linked below."
```

**Step one is quantisation:** storing each weight with fewer bits. A rough rule: **1 billion parameters at 8 bits is about 1 GB**, so the same model at 4 bits is half that. Mistral's guide calls 8-bit GGUF **"very close to lossless"** and marks 4-bit `q4_k_m` as a recommended middle ground. [(Mistral)](https://docs.mistral.ai/cookbooks/concept-deep-dive-quantization-methods-gguf)

**Step two is the runtime**, the engine that runs the file. On laptops that's often **llama.cpp**, which runs GGUF files with no Python or CUDA needed. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/docs/transformers/v5.14.0/community_integrations/llama_cpp) On phones, Google's **LiteRT-LM** runs Gemma 4 E2B on Android, iOS and the web; Google says it needs just **607 MB of memory on Apple mobile CPUs** and decodes about **52–56 tokens a second** on phone GPUs. [(Google)](https://developers.googleblog.com/en/blazing-fast-on-device-genai-with-litert-lm/) Apple's **Foundation Models framework** gives Swift apps the on-device model behind Apple Intelligence, **without internet**. [(Apple)](https://developer.apple.com/foundation-models-framework/)

**The real bottleneck is memory.** To write each word, the chip must read the model's active weights from memory. So **speed is mostly memory bandwidth**, and size is mostly RAM. That's why **mixture-of-experts** models help: only a few "experts" are read per word.


**Why now?** Inkling 2 Mini (story 1) brings near-frontier coding scores to a laptop. And AMD's Linux driver for **Ryzen AI NPUs** just reached 1.0, queued for Linux 7.5. NPUs are the chips laptop makers add for exactly this. [(Rio Rundown)](https://riorundown.substack.com/p/ai-news-nov-21-2026-enterprise-infrastructure)

<div class="box jargon" markdown="1">
**📖 Jargon buster**

- **Quantisation:** storing weights with fewer bits (16 → 4) so a model takes less memory.
- **NPU:** neural processing unit, a low-power chip built for AI maths.
- **GGUF:** a single-file model format used by llama.cpp, Ollama and others.
- **Memory bandwidth:** how fast the chip reads memory; it caps how many words per second you get.
</div>

<div class="box tryit" markdown="1">
**🧪 Try it in 10 minutes**

- **No code:** install **Ollama** on a laptop, pull a small model, then **switch Wi-Fi off** and ask it to summarise a paragraph. That's on-device AI.
- **Some code:** run the toy below with your own laptop's RAM and see which models fit.
</div>

<div class="box devs" markdown="1">
**🛠️ For devs: will it fit? 10 lines**

```python
MODELS = {"2B phone model": 2.0, "8B laptop model": 8.0, "26B (Inkling 2 Mini)": 26.4}
BITS = {"16-bit": 16, "8-bit": 8, "4-bit": 4}
RAM_GB = 32                       # a 32 GB laptop
for name, billions in MODELS.items():
    row = []
    for label, bits in BITS.items():
        gb = billions * bits / 8      # 1B params at 8 bits = 1 GB
        fits = "fits" if gb < RAM_GB * 0.75 else "too big"
        row.append(f"{label} {gb:5.1f} GB {fits}")
    print(f"{name:<22}", " | ".join(row))
# 26B: 16-bit 52.8 GB too big | 8-bit 26.4 GB too big | 4-bit 13.2 GB fits
```

The 75% rule leaves room for the OS and the **KV cache**, which grows with conversation length. Real files run bigger than this maths: Inkling 2 Mini's mixed 4- and 8-bit checkpoint is **about 20 GB**.

**Gotchas:** quality drops fastest below 4 bits; test on your own prompts. Long contexts eat RAM fast. And check the licence: "open weights" doesn't always mean free for business.
</div>

## Top story 1 · Inkling 2 Mini: an open model that loops its own brain, and fits on a laptop

<span class="tag hot">BIG DEAL</span><span class="tag try">TRY IT</span><span class="tag dev">DEV</span>

**What happened.** Thinking Machines Lab released **Inkling 2 Mini** on Friday under the **Apache 2.0** licence. It has **26.4B parameters, with 3.4B active per token**, takes text and images, and supports up to **1M tokens** of context. The company says it runs on **Apple silicon with 32 GB or more**. [(Thinking Machines)](https://thinkingmachines.ai/news/inkling-2-mini/) We covered its big sibling, Inkling 2 Small (594B), in Issue #39.

```viz:flow
title: "How Inkling 2 Mini's looped layers work"
headers: [In, Layers 0–5, Layers 6–17, Layers 18–23, Out]
columns:
  - [{label: Your prompt, sub: "text or image"}]
  - [{label: Run once, sub: "6 layers"}]
  - [{label: Pass 1, sub: "12 layers", style: accent}, {label: Pass 2, sub: "same layers again", style: accent}, {label: Pass 3, sub: "same layers again", style: accent}]
  - [{label: Run once, sub: "6 layers"}]
  - [{label: Next token, sub: "48 layer runs total"}]
note: "Our diagram of the layout in Thinking Machines' post and the Hugging Face blog: 24 stored layers, 6 + 3×12 + 6 = 48 layer runs per token."
```

<p style="break-after: avoid"><strong>The details that matter:</strong></p>

- **The loop:** the middle 12 layers run **three times** per token, so 24 stored layers do the work of 48. Each extra pass adds about **1B "effective" active parameters** (5.4B total) without more memory. [(Hugging Face)](https://huggingface.co/blog/inkling2mini)
- **The scores (company claims):** **79.2% on SWE-bench Verified**, **48.2% on DeepSWE**, and **20.1 on the Artificial Analysis Index**, tying Qwen3.8-27B. [(Thinking Machines)](https://thinkingmachines.ai/model-card/inkling-2-mini/)
- **Where to run it:** weights are on Hugging Face, with GGUF files for llama.cpp, MLX for Macs, plus Ollama, vLLM and SGLang. It's also on **Tinker** for fine-tuning.
- **Honest limits:** the model card says it knows less than bigger models, is **more likely to state wrong facts confidently**, can get stuck in reasoning loops, and **may delete files without asking**. Run agents in a sandbox.

**Why it matters.** Looping is a new way to **trade compute for memory**: more thinking per word without a bigger download. That's exactly the trade phones and laptops need.

## Top story 2 · AI-aided proof claims to crack the "real" Navier-Stokes problem

<span class="tag watch">SCIENCE</span><span class="tag watch">CLAIM</span>

**What happened.** NYU's **Tristan Buckmaster** and Anthropic's **Levent Alpöge** posted an AI-aided proof on Friday that they say shows **blowup in the Navier-Stokes fluid equations** without the special force OpenAI's September proof relied on. Many experts saw that force as **a loophole** in how the $1 million Clay prize problem is worded. [(Scientific American)](https://www.yahoo.com/news/science/articles/ai-just-solved-real-navier-170000805.html)

```viz:versus

right: {emoji: 💧, title: "Buckmaster & Alpöge (Nov 20)", points: ["No extra force, they claim", "79-page paper + Lean code", "Not yet reviewed by others"], example: "fluid on its own"}
note: "Simplified, from Scientific American's report and AI Weekly. 'Blowup' means a smooth flow that develops an infinite value in finite time."
```

<p style="break-after: avoid"><strong>The details that matter:</strong></p>

- **The package:** a **79-page paper** with a **600,000-line Lean 4** formalisation, built with a mix of Anthropic and OpenAI models including Codex. [(AI Weekly)](https://aiweekly.co/ai-news-today)
- **What's missing:** they rushed it out over **scoop fears** and haven't released the numerical **certificate** yet; they say it's coming soon. The write-up is "unpolished", by their own account.
- **The feud:** Buckmaster has accused OpenAI of trying to scoop his work; **OpenAI denies** it.
- **And a second claim:** Alpöge separately says he proved the **Generalized Riemann Hypothesis** with Claude's help, publishing **175,187 Lean files**. Early checks say they compile; the full proof still needs review. [(Rio Rundown)](https://riorundown.substack.com/p/ai-news-nov-21-2026-enterprise-infrastructure)

**Why it matters.** Lean (a proof checker) can confirm every step follows, but **humans still have to confirm the theorem says what's claimed**. Expect weeks of scrutiny before anyone calls this settled.

## Top story 3 · Signal blocks screenshots to keep AI agents out of your chats

<span class="tag watch">PRIVACY</span><span class="tag dev">DEV</span>

**What happened.** Signal is adding **screenshot blocking** to its Android and iOS apps, matching what its Windows app already does by default. President **Meredith Whittaker** called OS-level AI agents that "scan" your screen an **"existential threat"** to private messaging. [(TechCrunch)](https://techcrunch.com/2026/11/21/signal-restricts-mobile-screenshots-as-it-confronts-existential-threat-of-ai-surveillance/)

```viz:flow

  - [{label: Screen capture, sub: "AI agent 'reads' it", style: warn}, {label: Signal's block, sub: "screen stays blank", style: accent}]
  - [{label: Local database, sub: "or a remote server", style: soft}]
note: "Our diagram of Whittaker's argument as TechCrunch reports it. Encryption protects messages on the wire, not on a screen an agent can see."
```

<p style="break-after: avoid"><strong>The details that matter:</strong></p>

- **Her argument:** once an agent captures the screen, the data can be stored or sent to **whatever database or server the agent uses**, outside Signal's control and affecting **everyone in the chat**.
- **You can switch it off**, but Whittaker says doing so would **break Signal's privacy guarantees**.
- **She calls it incomplete:** agents have **"sneakier" ways** to reach app data that a screenshot block can't stop.
- **The context:** this week brought Gemini using your Mac (Issue #53) and reports of Meta's Muse reading Signal messages on Android (Issue #52).

**Why it matters.** It's the flip side of Part 1: **on-device AI is private from the cloud, but not from other apps**. Expect more apps to fight the OS for control of their own screens.
